Suvarna Myneni, AI-Native UX Designer, owned UX/UI design: end-to-end flows, prototyping and the UI kit, with Cubic product, engineering and back-office teams.

03

Problem

A sale counter in someone else’s shop.

The app had to make a fare transaction quick and hard to get wrong for a shop clerk, and it also had to cover who may log in, which device belongs to which shop, how a shift opens and closes, and how money is reconciled and refunded.

Commuters in South East Queensland can buy and top up a go card at hundreds of newsagents, convenience stores and other shops. Translink lists over 680 places to buy a card and over 1,600 places to top one up. Cubic Transportation Systems operates and maintains the go card system. At the counter, the transaction happens on a retailer device, run by a shop clerk whose main job is not transport ticketing.

What the app has to do

  • Read a go card and show its balance, passes and last transaction.
  • Add value or a pass to an existing card, or issue a new card with its deposit and issuance fee.
  • Take cash or credit/debit, then write the purchase onto the card.
  • Refund the last transaction or a ticket, and print receipts.
  • Run shifts, daily and monthly reports, staff accounts and device registration.

Why that is hard

  • Payment and loading the card are two separate steps. If the card is not loaded after payment, the customer has paid for nothing.
  • Three roles share one device and need different permissions.
  • Security events such as a deactivated device, a suspended account or a forced PIN reset have to be clear to a clerk without help.
  • Clerks change often, so the app has to teach itself: there is a full in-app training mode.

End Users

Three roles on one device. The Home/Menu frame (HO1) gives each one a different set of tiles.

Notes on the board set the boundaries: Register Device is “completed by Admin only”, and for Forgot PIN, “Clerks contact Local Admin for PIN resets.”

Admin

Store setup and authority

Transact, Tickets, Last Transaction, Reports, Manage Accounts, Manage Shifts, Settings, Log Out. Activates the admin account, registers the device to the business, manages users and settings, resets PINs.

Manager

Counter and roster

Transact, Tickets, Last Transaction, Reports, Manage Accounts, Manage Shifts, Log Out. Runs the counter, manages staff accounts and shifts, and can reset a clerk’s PIN.

Clerk

The sale

Transact, Tickets, Last Transaction, Reports, Shift Reports, Log Out. Serves customers: sells, tops up, refunds, prints receipts and ends the shift.

Research Methods

Workshops and counter workflows with Cubic, then public sources on the go card retail channel. Sources were checked against the pages listed below.

Workshops

Stakeholder workshops

Working sessions with Cubic product, engineering and back-office teams to map the requirements, the three roles, the configuration options and what the APIs could and could not report.

Key insight: authority sits with a few people. PIN resets, device registration and account changes belong to admins and managers. Clerks need messages that tell them who to ask.

Workflow

Retailer workflow review

A review of how a go card sale fits into a shop counter: serving a queue, taking cash or card, handing over a receipt, and reconciling takings at the end of a shift.

Key insight: the shift is the unit of accounting. Clerks need totals by transaction type and by payment type that they can check against the till.

Walkthrough

Prototype walkthroughs

Walkthroughs of the wired flows with the Cubic teams, screen by screen, including the error and alternate paths, to check the logic before build.

Key insight: payment and loading are separate steps. The point between them carries the most risk, so it needs an explicit way to recover.

Desk research

Public sources

Translink, Cubic, Queensland Government and peer-network pages describing where go cards are sold, what a shop can do, and how long the card channel is expected to run.

Key insight: staff change often, and help has to live inside the app. Errors are also limited by what the API returns, so messages have to be honest about what the system knows.

Questions the screens had to answer

  • What happens at the counter when a payment succeeds but the card cannot be loaded?
  • How do clerks reconcile cash and card takings at the end of a shift?
  • Who resets a forgotten PIN, and what does a clerk do when nobody senior is there?
  • How does a new clerk learn the app without formal training?
  • When do refunds happen, and how are they recorded?

Insights from the flows and workflows

  • Payment and loading are separate steps. The point between them carries the most risk, so it needs an explicit way to recover.
  • Authority sits with a few people. PIN resets, device registration and account changes belong to admins and managers. Clerks need clear messages that tell them who to ask.
  • The shift is the unit of accounting. Clerks need totals by transaction type and by payment type that they can check against the till.
  • Staff change often. Help has to live inside the app, not in a manual.
  • Errors are limited by what the API returns. Error messages have to be honest about what the system actually knows.

What is public about the go card retail channel

The network

  • Translink states that its go card system “is operated and maintained by Cubic Transportation Systems.” [1]
  • A go card can be bought at over 680 locations and topped up at over 1,600 across South East Queensland, including selected retailers. [2]
  • At retailers, “card types accepted are at the discretion of the retailer.” Buying a card carries a refundable deposit of $10 (adult) or $5 (child, concession, senior). [2]
  • Retailer listings show the services each outlet offers. The 7-Eleven outlets checked list Buy go card, Top up go card and Change expiry date. [3] Queensland Government open data publishes the full go card retailer list. [4]
  • Retailers that only sell pre-loaded cards are not given top-up facilities. Retailer applications go to a Cubic address. [5]

The history and what is changing

  • Cubic’s 2014 release says the go card system went live in 2008, with Cubic as integrator and supplier. Its NextLink technology let retailers connect their own point-of-sale equipment to Cubic’s central system, so up to 50 more 7-Eleven stores could join. [6]
  • Cubic won an AU$371 million Smart Ticketing contract in 2018, covering account-based ticketing and open-loop payments. [7]
  • Contactless Visa and Mastercard payment now works on all SEQ trains, trams, ferries and buses. [8]
  • The Queensland Government says customers can keep using go cards until a new reloadable card is developed. It also says go card and Smart Ticketing will run side by side until the end of 2027. [9] [10]

Why it matters for the design. The counter will keep serving people who use a card rather than a phone or bank card: tourists buying a pass, children and concession holders, and people paying cash. That is why the app is built around reading a card, cash and card payment, passes, and printed receipts.

Patterns worth keeping

  • A top-up at the counter works straight away. That is the reason to go to a shop instead of topping up online.
  • Retailers offer different service sets, so the app has to show only what this outlet and this role can do.
  • Official branded equipment is how customers know a shop is genuine, so the device has to look like Translink.

Where Translink Merchant goes further

  • A guided way to recover when payment is taken but the card is not yet loaded.
  • Different permissions for Admin, Manager and Clerk on the same device.
  • Training built into every key screen.

User Personas

Representative proto-personas for the admin, the manager and the clerk. Built from the role rules and permissions in the screens, not from named research participants.

AD

Admin · Store owner

Owns a newsagent that also sells go cards. Sets the device up once, adds staff, and handles anything that needs authority.

Goals. Register the device to the right business and address, control who can transact, see daily and monthly takings.

Frustrations. Being called in for every forgotten PIN, and a locked device when the shop is busy.

Flows. LO2, LO3, MA1, RE1.

MG

Manager · Shift lead

Runs the counter and the roster. Covers when clerks are stuck and makes sure takings match at the end of the day.

Goals. Reset a clerk’s PIN, suspend or end shifts, check refunds.

Frustrations. Shift totals that don’t match the till, and refunds that are hard to trace.

Flows. LO4, MA1, MC1, RE1.

CL

Clerk · Counter staff

Often casual or new, and serving a queue. Sells newspapers and snacks too. Ticketing is a small part of the job.

Goals. Top up or sell a pass in seconds, take cash or card, print a receipt, end the shift cleanly.

Frustrations. Unclear errors, and not knowing whether the card was actually loaded.

Flows. TA1–TA4, MC1, LO4, UT1.

Design Decisions

Decisions that are visible in the screens.

Card first, then cart

Every sale starts with Transact → Read card. Manage Card then shows the balance, passes and last transaction before the clerk chooses Top Up or Add Pass. Items go into a cart, so one sale can hold several products.

Trade-off: the sale starts with a read, so the clerk sees the card before taking money.

One home grid, filtered by role

Admin, Manager and Clerk all see the same tile layout and slide-out menu, with only the tiles their role allows. Training one role prepares people for the others.

Trade-off: roles share one layout, so a person moving from clerk to manager already knows the grid.

Never take money without loading the card

After payment, the clerk is sent to Load Card. Cancelling there opens a choice: “Refund” cancels the transaction and starts a refund, “Dismiss” goes back to loading.

Trade-off: Load Card cannot be waved away. The clerk has to refund or go back to loading.

A cash check step

For cash, a Confirm Payment screen asks the clerk to verify they have collected the money before the card is loaded. A note says this screen appears only when the payment method is cash.

Trade-off: cash gains a confirmation screen that card payments do not show.

Configurable payment and limits

Notes record that credit/debit can use an integrated payment app or an external POS device, and that the minimum and maximum top-up amounts are configurable. An inline error shows the allowed range.

Trade-off: the UI does not hard-code one payment device or one amount range. It shows the range the configuration allows.

Errors that match the API

The API reports a wrong customer ID, but it does not say whether the User ID or the PIN is wrong. So the design shows a single “Incorrect User ID or PIN” and highlights both fields.

Trade-off: the message cannot point at one field, because the API does not know which one failed.

Refunds read as negative

History and reports show refunds with a minus sign. The board explains this as the standard convention: a refund lowers the merchant’s takings, for example −$50.

Trade-off: a refund looks like a negative taking, so the shift total still adds up.

Plain security states

Separate full-screen dialogs for Device Deactivated, Account Not Active, PIN Reset Required and Auto-Logout. Each says what happened and who to contact.

Trade-off: each security event takes over the screen, with one action, instead of a short toast.

Shifts with a paper trail

End Shift prints a report with totals by transaction type and by payment type. Suspend Shift lets a clerk step away without closing the day.

Trade-off: ending a shift always prints. Suspending does not close the day’s takings.

Training built in

UT1 pairs every key screen with a Training overlay that explains it. New staff learn on the real interface.

Trade-off: training uses the live screens, so there is no separate manual to keep in step with the app.

Type and touch

The board does not name a typeface. Type, spacing and component sizes follow Android conventions for a handheld. One-handed use and large touch targets set the tile and button sizes. On screen, the hierarchy is a short tile or dialog title, one plain sentence, and a single action. Printed receipts and shift reports use a shared layout: retailer, device and user IDs, then lines by transaction type and payment type. The figures in those receipts are sample data.

Final Design

Thirteen flows, from getting in to learning the app.

LO1

Log In

Splash, 6-digit User ID and PIN with a numeric keypad, role-based landing. Errors for out of service, incorrect entry and empty fields, plus dialogs for device and account states. Connection errors for the internet and the back office.

LO2

Login › Forgot PIN

Admin only: password recovery, a security question, a verification code, a new PIN. Error states for a wrong answer, a wrong code and a PIN mismatch.

LO3

Login › Register Device

Admin only: Retailer ID, User ID and PIN, then business and physical address, then success. Also Activate Admin Account.

LO4

Log Out / End Shift

Log out per role, end shift with a printed shift report, suspend shift, auto log-out.

HO1

Home/Menu

Home screens for Admin, Manager and Clerk, and the slide-out menu.

UT1

In-App User Training

A Training overlay for every key screen, from Log In to New User Account.

MC1

Manage Card

Balances and passes, print card details, history (transactions and journeys, existing and new design), refund the last transaction, transactions with several products, error screens.

TA1

Add Value to Existing Card

Top up with cash or credit/debit, load card, receipt.

TA2

Add Pass to Existing Card

Pick a pass (for example go explore), cart, cash or credit/debit.

TA3

Add Value to New Card

New card with issuance fee and deposit, top up, payment, load card, receipts, error screens.

TA4

Add Pass to New Card

A new card with a pass, error screens.

MA1

Outcome

What shipped in the design, and what the work made clear.

Delivered

  • End-to-end designs for 13 flows. Access, home, selling, card management, reporting, accounts and training, each with its error and edge states.
  • A role-based experience. One layout for Admin, Manager and Clerk, with permissions applied the same way across the home screen, menu and flows.
  • Built-in training. A Training overlay for every key screen, so new staff learn on the real interface.
  • An error-recovery model. Refund-or-Dismiss when loading is cancelled, a cash check step, plain security dialogs, and errors written from what the API actually returns.
  • A developer-ready specification. Notes on API behaviour and configuration sit next to the screens they affect.

What I learned

  • On a shared counter device, errors are designed from what the API can actually report, not from the ideal message.
  • The riskiest moment is between payment and loading the card, so that step gets the most protection.
  • A tile grid filtered by role is easier to train and support than separate apps for each role.
  • Built-in training does work a manual would otherwise have to do for staff who change often.
  • Annotating API limits on the board saves rework later, because developers and designers read the same source.

Public sources say go card and Smart Ticketing run side by side until the end of 2027, so the retailer counter keeps serving people who pay with a card or with cash. The flows are built for that counter: read a card, take cash or credit/debit, load it, print a receipt, and close the shift.
